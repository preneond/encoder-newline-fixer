·Gap classification, not generation

Text is split into whitespace-delimited words. Between each consecutive pair sits exactly one gap, and the model assigns it one of four separators. Reconstruction interleaves the original words with the predicted separators — a token-classification head, not a decoder.

Input newlines are deliberately ignored — the problem statement says they are unreliable, so the text is re-tokenized on all whitespace and the worst case (every newline wrong) becomes identical to the average case. Long documents run through overlapping sliding windows stitched on their central regions.

¶The labels were free

Clean, well-formatted text already contains the answer. Structure is destroyed programmatically; the destroyed copy is the input, the original is the label. No annotation budget.

Data diligence that mattered: Salesforce/wikitext "raw" was probed first and rejected — the variant is word-tokenized ( @-@  joiners, split contractions) and would have poisoned training.

·Exploration: three axes, one protocol

Every claim below is a measurement, not an opinion — same seeds, same test set.

1 · Learning rate: the conventional wisdom left 2.6 points on the table

2 · Distillation: upgrading the tiny model for free at inference time

The fine-tuned encoder was frozen as a teacher and the 2.4M-param BiLSTM retrained with per-gap soft targets: loss = (1−α)·CE + α·τ²·KL, τ = 2. Balanced hard+soft (α = 0.5) buys +4.3 test macro-F1 at identical size and speed — soft targets carry inter-class structure a hard label can’t (“NEWLINE-or-PARA, certainly not SPACE”). Nearly all-soft (α = 0.9) gives the gain back: the hard-label term anchors the rare-class boundaries.

3 · Backbone sweep: the frontier around the served model

The judgment call: roberta-base wins on every quality metric — and it was not promoted. It falls short of a clear win once serving enters the frame: 1.5× the parameters, half the throughput, and a training path that cannot run on Apple-silicon GPUs. distilroberta-base stays the served model — the best quality-per-millisecond on the frontier.

↵The debugging story worth telling

roberta-base refused to fine-tune on this machine: training collapsed to NaN within 50 steps, twice, at two different learning rates. Hypothesis-driven isolation, one experiment per step:

  1. Identical collapse at lr 1e-4 and lr 2e-5.

    Two learning rates, bit-identical wreckage → not a hyperparameter. Something deterministic poisons the run regardless of step size.

  2. Loss finite. Gradients finite. Weights NaN — right after optimizer.step().

    An instrumented copy of the trainer checked every tensor every step: the corruption enters through the AdamW update itself, from mathematically valid inputs.

  3. clip_grad_norm_ reports a total norm of 0.0 while the largest single gradient is 4.86.

    A norm can never be smaller than its largest element. The MPS kernels are returning garbage — provably, not speculatively.

  4. Adding a per-step .item() sync makes the NaN vanish.

    Forced synchronization between backward, clip, and step hides the failure → an asynchronous command-buffer race in the torch 2.13 MPS backend, triggered by this model’s 199-tensor parameter set. The smaller backbones never hit it.

  5. Resolution fixed

    roberta-base was trained on CPU with the identical recipe; both trainers now abort on a non-finite loss instead of saving a corrupt checkpoint.

Conclusion. Did we use an existing model? Yes — the winner is a fine-tune of one. On this task, at this data scale, transfer learning dominates training from scratch on quality (0.815 vs 0.632), data-efficiency (4× less data), and — at batch-1 GPU inference — even speed. Two cheap wins came from systematic exploration: the lr sweep (+2.6 F1 free) and distillation (+4.3 F1 for the tiny model). The backbone sweep confirmed the serving choice rather than changing it.
